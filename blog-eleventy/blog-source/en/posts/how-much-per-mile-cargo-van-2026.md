<p class="text-lg md:text-xl text-gray-200 font-medium mb-6">
  If you drive a cargo van and someone asks "how much are you making per mile?", there's a good chance the number you give them isn't your real number. Not because you're lying — because almost nobody calculates per-mile pay correctly. In this post I'll give you the real 2026 ranges, explain why the number you see on the load board almost never equals what you actually take home, and show you how to know whether a load is worth accepting before you say yes.
</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">💰 What Is Revenue Per Mile (RPM) Really?</h2>
<p>RPM stands for Revenue Per Mile — what a load pays you divided by the miles you drive. Sounds simple. The problem is that almost every carrier calculates RPM using only the loaded miles (pickup → delivery), ignoring deadhead: the miles you drive to reach the pickup and the miles you need to reposition after delivery. Those miles don't pay, but they burn fuel, wear your van, and consume your time. If you don't count them, your "real" RPM is a fiction.</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">📊 Real Per-Mile Pay Ranges in 2026 (Cargo Van / Sprinter)</h2>
<p>These are reference ranges based on typical expedite loads for cargo van and Sprinter across the United States in 2026:</p>
<ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-2 my-4">
  <li><strong>Low RPM (avoid):</strong> $0.70 – $0.90/mile — rarely covers your real operating cost, especially once you add deadhead.</li>
  <li><strong>Acceptable RPM (context-dependent):</strong> $0.90 – $1.20/mile — can work if deadhead is low and the delivery area has solid outbound freight.</li>
  <li><strong>Good RPM:</strong> $1.20 – $1.50/mile — real profit margin for most operators.</li>
  <li><strong>Excellent RPM:</strong> $1.50+/mile — uncommon, usually hot shot or urgent loads, or short high-demand lanes.</li>
</ul>
<p>These numbers shift by region, van type (cargo van vs. Sprinter), and season — but they serve as a starting point to know whether a load is even in the right range before running deeper calculations.</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">⚠️ Why the Pay You See on the Load Board Isn't Your Real Pay</h2>
<p>The broker shows you pay divided by loaded miles. That's gross RPM. Your real RPM needs to account for:</p>
<ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-2 my-4">
  <li><strong>Deadhead</strong> — miles to reach the pickup.</li>
  <li><strong>Repositioning</strong> — miles after delivery to reach the next zone with freight.</li>
  <li><strong>Real cost per mile</strong> — fuel, maintenance, depreciation, insurance — not just gas.</li>
</ul>
<p>The correct formula:</p>
<div class="bg-white/5 border border-white/10 rounded-xl p-4 font-mono text-cyan-400 my-4 text-center">
  REAL RPM = Total Pay ÷ (Deadhead + Loaded Miles + Repositioning)
</div>
<p>A quick example: a load pays $1,200 for 800 loaded miles ($1.50/mile on the load board). But you need 120 miles of deadhead to reach the pickup and roughly 180 miles of repositioning to get to the next freight zone. Your real RPM is $1,200 ÷ 1,100 = <strong class="text-cyan-400">$1.09/mile</strong> — not $1.50. That $0.41/mile gap, multiplied by your monthly mileage, is the difference between a good month and a month where you can't figure out where your money went.</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">🎯 What Is Your Minimum RPM? (It's Not the Same for Everyone)</h2>
<p>There's no universal magic number — your minimum RPM depends on your real cost per mile, which varies by van, route, and how you operate. As a rule of thumb: your floor RPM should be your actual cost per mile plus a profit margin of at least 20–30%. If your cost per mile is $0.85, accepting loads below $1.05–$1.10/mile means you're working with almost no real profit — even if the numbers on paper look like you're ahead.</p>
