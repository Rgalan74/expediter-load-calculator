faqs:
- question: Can I switch methods from one year to the next?
  answer: It depends. If you choose standard mileage the first year you use the van for business, you can switch to actual expenses in later years. But if you start with actual expenses (or use accelerated depreciation like Section 179), you generally can't switch to standard mileage for that same van afterward. That's why the first-year choice matters so much.
- question: Which method is better for a new, financed van?
  answer: There's no fixed rule, but when the monthly payment, loan interest, and depreciation are high, actual expenses usually produces a bigger deduction than standard mileage. Still, it depends on your specific numbers — it's worth running both scenarios before deciding.
- question: Do I still need a mileage log if I use actual expenses?
  answer: Yes. With actual expenses you still need your business-use percentage (business miles ÷ total miles for the year) to prorate every expense. Without that log, you have no way to support what portion of fuel, insurance, or depreciation belongs to the business.

  answer: This article explains how both methods work, but it isn't a substitute for professional tax advice. <p class="text-lg md:text-xl text-gray-200 font-medium mb-6">
  Every mile you drive with a load has a real cost — and part of that cost can be deducted from your taxes. The
  question isn't <em>whether</em> you can deduct the use of your cargo van, it's <strong>which method</strong>
  gets you the bigger deduction: standard mileage or actual expenses.
</p>

<p>
  Choosing wrong isn't just leaving money on the table — in some cases, choosing wrong in year one locks you out
  of the other option for good, for that same van. Let's walk through how each method works, so you can bring
  your accountant the right question instead of a generic one.
</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">🚐 Method 1: Standard Mileage</h2>
<p>
  With this method, you multiply the business miles you drove during the year by the rate the IRS publishes
  annually (it changes every year, so always check the current rate at
  <a href="https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/standard-mileage-rates" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-cyan-400 hover:underline">irs.gov</a>
  before you calculate). That figure already bakes in estimated wear, maintenance, and depreciation — they
  aren't added separately.
</p>

<div class="callout tip">
  <strong class="text-cyan-400">💡 Advantages:</strong>
  <ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-1 mt-2">
    <li><strong>Simplicity:</strong> you only need a reliable business-mileage log, not every fuel or
      maintenance receipt.</li>
    <li><strong>Predictable:</strong> you know exactly what every mile is worth from day one of the year.</li>
    <li><strong>Less paperwork:</strong> ideal if your van is already paid off and you don't have large
      financing costs or accelerated depreciation.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div class="callout warning">
  <strong class="text-orange-400 font-bold">⚠️ Restrictions you need to know</strong>
  <p>
    Standard mileage is generally <strong>not available</strong> if you've already claimed accelerated
    depreciation (like Section 179 or bonus depreciation) on that van, or if you operate a fleet of 5 or more
    vehicles at the same time. The choice is also made in the <strong>first year</strong> you use the vehicle
    for business — if you start with actual expenses, you typically can't switch to standard mileage for that
    same van later.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">🧾 Method 2: Actual Expenses</h2>
<p>
  Here you deduct the real cost of operating the van, prorated by your business-use percentage (business miles
  ÷ total miles for the year). There's no fixed rate — it depends on what you actually spent.
</p>

<div class="callout example">
  <strong class="text-green-400">✅ Expenses typically included:</strong>
  <ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-1 mt-2">
    <li><strong>Depreciation</strong> on the van's value (or lease payments, if you're leasing).</li>
    <li><strong>Loan interest</strong>, if you financed it.</li>
    <li><strong>Fuel, oil, and regular maintenance.</strong></li>
    <li><strong>Commercial insurance and registration/plates.</strong></li>
    <li><strong>Repairs and tires.</strong></li>
    <li><strong>Tolls and parking</strong> tied to loads (these are deducted separately, at 100%, regardless of
      which method you choose).</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">🧮 How to Start Deciding</h2>
<p>
  There's no universal answer, but one question points you in the right direction: <strong>how much are you
  really paying to keep that van on the road?</strong> If the monthly payment, interest, and maintenance are
  high, it's worth running both methods before filing — sometimes the difference is thousands of dollars in
  deductions. If your van is already paid off and expenses are low, standard mileage is usually simpler and,
  often, just as good.
</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">📝 The Log You Need Either Way</h2>
<p>
  No matter which method you choose, the IRS expects a reliable mileage log: date, origin, destination, purpose
  of the trip, and miles driven. <strong>Smart Load Solution's</strong> load history already stores the origin,
  destination, and miles for every load you accepted — it's the foundation you can hand your accountant instead
  of reconstructing the whole year from memory.
</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">🏆 Bottom Line</h2>
<p>
  Neither method is "the best" in the abstract — the best one is whichever fits your van, your financing, and
  your actual expenses this year. What is universal is the need to keep good records from day one, because
  that's the only way to compare both methods with real numbers instead of guessing.
</p>
