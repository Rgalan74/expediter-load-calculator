<p class="text-lg md:text-xl text-gray-200 font-medium mb-6">
  If you finish the month wondering where your money went — even though you drove plenty of miles and took what looked like solid loads — the problem probably isn't the loads. It's the deadhead you never counted. Deadhead miles are the single largest silent expense in the expediting business, and almost no one measures them correctly. Here I'll explain exactly what they are, how much they're actually costing you, and how to start accounting for them in every decision you make.
</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">👻 What Are Deadhead Miles?</h2>
<p>Deadhead miles are the miles you drive without a load and without anyone paying you for them. There are two moments where they show up:</p>
<ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-2 my-4">
  <li><strong>Inbound deadhead:</strong> the miles from your current location to the load's pickup point.</li>
  <li><strong>Repositioning (outbound deadhead):</strong> the miles from where you delivered to the next zone where freight is available.</li>
</ul>
<p>Neither is covered by the broker. But both cost you exactly the same as loaded miles: fuel, van wear, time. The difference is that one generates income and the other doesn't — yet most carriers only calculate RPM using their loaded miles, ignoring deadhead entirely.</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">🕵️ Why Deadhead Is the "Silent Thief"</h2>
<p>It's not a lump-sum expense that hits you like a big invoice. It's a constant drip, load after load, accumulating without you noticing. A real example: if your calculation error from not counting deadhead correctly is $0.40/mile, and you drive 4,000 miles a month, that error costs you <strong class="text-orange-400">$1,600 per month</strong> — nearly $19,200 per year. And the worst part: the whole time, you believe you're earning more than you actually are, because your mental RPM never included the deadhead.</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">📐 How to Calculate the Real Impact of Deadhead</h2>
<p>The formula is simple once you have it clear:</p>
<div class="bg-white/5 border border-white/10 rounded-xl p-4 font-mono text-cyan-400 my-4 text-center">
  REAL RPM = Total Pay ÷ (Deadhead + Loaded Miles + Repositioning)
</div>
<p>Example: a load pays $1,000 for 700 loaded miles. If you only look at that, your RPM appears to be $1.43/mile. But you need to drive 100 miles of deadhead to reach the pickup, and after delivery, another 150 miles of repositioning to reach a zone with freight. Your real RPM is $1,000 ÷ 950 = <strong class="text-cyan-400">$1.05/mile</strong> — 26% less than you thought.</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">🚦 How Much Deadhead Is "Too Much"?</h2>
<p>There's no universal fixed number, but a practical rule: if your combined deadhead plus repositioning exceeds 25–30% of your loaded miles, it's time to seriously question whether that load is worth it — regardless of how good the gross rate looks. A 50-mile deadhead for a 600-mile load is manageable. A 150-mile deadhead for a 300-mile load can turn a "good" load into one that barely covers your expenses.</p>

<h2 class="text-xl md:text-2xl font-bold text-white mt-8 mb-4">✅ How to Reduce the Impact of Deadhead (Without Turning Down Loads)</h2>
<ol class="list-decimal pl-6 space-y-2 my-4">
  <li><strong>Think in zones, not individual loads.</strong> Before accepting, ask yourself where that load takes you and how easy it is to find outbound freight from there.</li>
  <li><strong>Negotiate deadhead pay when possible.</strong> Some brokers will adjust the rate if the deadhead is high — and you give them the exact mileage number, not a generic complaint.</li>
  <li><strong>Evaluate the full load cycle, not just the paid leg.</strong> A load with solid gross RPM that drops you in a freight dead zone can end up costing more in the next deadhead than you earned on this one.</li>
</ol>
