Module 3 · Lesson 2

States to go into and states to get out of

8 min read Strategy Free
What you will learn
  1. Why some zones are easy to get into and hard to get out of.
  2. How to calculate cycle RPM.
  3. When the app warns you about a hard-to-leave zone.

01Direction matters

Many places receive more freight than they send. Delivering there is easy; getting out is not. It is common in border cities like Laredo and in end-of-the-road areas like South Florida. The opposite happens in industrial areas: more freight leaves than comes in.

02The in-and-out matrix

Good to get in and outYour circuit. Accept normal rates
Good to get in, bad to get outCharge more to go in: the way back will cost you
Bad to get in, good to get outYou can accept a bit less to get there: it positions you well
Bad both waysAvoid it unless the pay covers everything

03Cycle RPM

When you deliver into a hard zone, the load does not end at delivery. It ends when you leave with the next one. That is why you should measure the cycle: this load plus a typical outbound one.

Cycle RPM = (inbound pay + typical outbound pay) ÷ (inbound miles + outbound miles)

04The app warns you

If you have 3 or more loads leaving a state in the last 12 months and their average RPM is below your cost, or below 85% of your overall average, the calculator flags it as a hard-to-leave zone when you deliver there. It shows you the cycle RPM and profit.

That warning also raises the minimum for short loads delivering into that zone: from $150 to $250 profit for a van.

To negotiate

"Laredo leaves me with little outbound freight. For the whole trip to work I need more on the way in." If the cycle RPM does not reach your threshold, ask for the difference on the inbound load.

In summary

  • A load ends when you leave with the next one, not at delivery.
  • Measure the cycle: inbound + a typical outbound load.
  • The app flags a hard zone when your outbound loads pay little.
