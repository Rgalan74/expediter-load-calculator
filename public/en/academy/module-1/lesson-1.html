Module 1 · Lesson 1

What real RPM is (and why most drivers get it wrong)

6 min read Fundamentals Free
What you will learn
  1. The difference between apparent RPM and real RPM.
  2. Which miles count: empty, loaded and repositioning.
  3. How the app calculator handles it.

01The most common mistake

Almost everyone calculates RPM like this:

Total pay ÷ loaded miles = RPM
Why it is wrong

It only counts the miles that pay you. The miles you drive empty also cost you fuel, wear and time.

02The correct formula

Real RPM = total pay ÷ (empty miles + loaded miles + repositioning miles)
Empty miles (deadhead)From where you are to the pickup
Loaded milesFrom pickup to delivery: the only ones that pay
RepositioningFrom delivery to the next zone with good freight

03An example with real miles

You are in Toledo, OH. You are offered a pickup in Detroit (59 empty miles) and a delivery in Oklahoma City (1,027 loaded miles) for $1,500. Oklahoma City has little outbound freight, so afterwards you move to Dallas (206 miles).

Three ways to calculate the same RPM
Loaded miles only$1,500 ÷ 1,027$1.46 /mi
Loaded + empty$1,500 ÷ 1,086$1.38 /mi
Real RPM: all miles$1,500 ÷ 1,292$1.16 /mi
$0.30
per mile between what you think you make and what you make: about $387 on this load
Watch out

If your real cost is $0.53 per mile (you calculate it in lesson 2), the apparent RPM makes you think you keep $0.93 per mile. You actually keep $0.63.

The load that pays more, $2,000, is the one that leaves you the least per mile. Atlanta leaves you 71% more for every mile you drive.

06In the app

Apply it in the app

The calculator already counts all miles. Enter the miles to the pickup in Empty Miles and, if you know you will have to move empty after delivery, add those too. The panel RPM uses total miles.

Open the calculator →

To decide where to reposition, open Zones: it shows which states have given you the best RPM and profit based on your history.

In summary

  • Real RPM divides the pay by all miles, not just loaded miles.
  • Empty and repositioning miles change the result more than it seems.
  • A load that pays more can leave you less per mile.
