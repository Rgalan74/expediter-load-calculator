Module 2 · Lesson 2

Loaded and empty miles: one single trip

8 min read Practice Free
What you will learn
  1. Why empty miles count the same as loaded miles.
  2. How to calculate the empty-mile percentage.
  3. How much extra pay to ask for more empty miles.

01One trip, not two

Empty miles are not an "extra". They are part of the same job: to earn the rate you have to drive them, burn fuel and use hours of your day.

Common thinking

"It pays $700 for 356 miles: almost $2 a mile. The empty part is separate."

Correct thinking

"To earn $700 I have to drive 639 miles. That is $1.10 a mile."

02The empty-mile percentage

Empty % = empty miles ÷ total miles × 100

There is no magic number, but it works as a warning: under 10–15% is normal; above 25% the rate has to make up for a lot of empty driving. The calculator does not show this percentage; what it does is put every mile into the RPM and the costs.

04How much more to ask for extra empty miles

Every empty mile costs you the same as a loaded one. So it does not pull you below your target, each extra mile should be paid at least at your target RPM:

Minimum extra pay = extra empty miles × target RPM

With a $0.55 cost and a 20% margin, the target RPM is $0.55 ÷ 0.80 = $0.69. If the pickup is 100 miles farther than usual, ask for at least $69 more.

06How to cut empty miles

  • Deliver where there is outbound freight. A slightly lower rate into a good zone can leave you better off than a high one into a zone with no freight.
  • Prefer nearby pickups. With a similar RPM, the load with fewer empty miles almost always wins.
  • Check Zones. It shows which states have given you the best RPM and profit based on your history.
  • Always enter empty miles in the calculator. If you leave them at 0, the panel shows a better load than it really is.

In summary

  • Empty and loaded miles are one trip: real RPM uses both.
  • A load with many empty miles can leave less per hour even if it pays more.
  • For every extra empty mile, ask for at least your target RPM.
